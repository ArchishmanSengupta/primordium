Primordium is an experiment based on research from Google DeepMind. We start with hundreds of tiny random programs, let them interact, and watch to see if self-replicating "organisms" spontaneously emerge — with no design, no mutation, and no fitness function.

00 — Background

How it works

BrainFuck is one of the simplest programming languages ever designed. It has only 7 instructions — move a pointer left or right, increment or decrement a value, start or end a loop, and copy a byte. Despite this simplicity, it is Turing complete, meaning it can theoretically compute anything a modern computer can. Each instruction is a single character, and every other byte value is treated as a no-op (ignored during execution).

The "soup" is a pool of small byte arrays — think of them as tiny programs. At the start of the experiment, every program is filled with completely random bytes. Most of these bytes aren't valid BrainFuck instructions at all. It's pure noise: no design, no structure, no intent. The pool typically contains a few hundred programs, each a few dozen bytes long.

An interaction works like this: pick two programs at random, concatenate them end-to-end into one long tape, run that combined tape as a single BrainFuck program (with a step limit to prevent infinite loops), then split the tape back at the midpoint. The two halves go back into the pool, possibly changed by execution. Repeat this millions of times.

Imagine a pool of random LEGO instruction booklets. Most are gibberish — nonsense pages that don't build anything. But occasionally, by pure accident, a booklet contains instructions that say "copy these pages into the other booklet." That booklet now has a twin, and both can copy themselves further. Over millions of random pairings, the self-copying booklets crowd out the gibberish. No one designed them. No one selected for them. They simply persisted because they replicate — and that's the most basic form of natural selection. This is thermodynamic selection: no fitness function, no mutation operator, no genetic algorithm. Just physics-like dynamics and self-organization.

The key insight: Code and data live on the same tape. When two programs merge and run, they can accidentally copy bytes from one half into the other. If a program happens to copy itself into its partner, it gets replicated — and now there are more copies of it in the pool. This is natural selection with no designer involved.
The 3 criteria for "life": We say life has emerged when all three conditions are met simultaneously. (1) Structure: the average instruction density is at least 10% — meaning the programs contain significantly more valid code than random chance (~2.7%). (2) Replication: at least 5% of programs in the pool are exact copies of another program. (3) Purpose: the compression ratio of programs drops below 0.8, meaning they have internal structure and repetition — they aren't random noise anymore.
01 — The 7 Instructions

The complete BrainFuck language

Everything that happens in this experiment is built from just these 7 instructions. That's the entire language. Every other byte value (there are 249 of them) does nothing at all — it's treated as a no-op. In a random byte array, only about 2.7% of bytes will be valid instructions by chance. When that number rises far above 2.7%, something interesting is happening.

What to look for: The colors below match the colors used throughout this visualization. Blue instructions move the pointer, green instructions change values, red instructions create loops, and gold is the copy instruction — arguably the most important one, since it lets programs write bytes into each other.

04 — The Rule

Merge, run, split

This diagram shows exactly what happens in a single interaction. Two programs (Tape A and Tape B) are joined end-to-end, executed as one BrainFuck program, then split back at the midpoint. The colored cells are valid instructions; grey is noise.

The instruction pointer (the red triangle labeled IP) starts at the left edge of the combined tape and walks right, executing each instruction it encounters. As it runs, it can write bytes into any position on the tape via the . instruction. This is the only mechanism by which one program can modify another. There is no mutation operator, no crossover, no fitness evaluation. Programs simply rewrite each other through execution — and those that happen to write copies of themselves persist.

Why this matters: The instruction pointer walks across the combined tape executing code. As it runs, it can write bytes from one half into the other. That's the only mechanism — no mutation, no crossover, no fitness function. Just programs accidentally rewriting each other. The split point is always at the exact midpoint, so both programs maintain their original size.

05 — Instruction Distribution

Which instructions survive?

Not all instructions are equally useful for replication. This chart compares the count of each instruction type at the start of the experiment (light bars) versus the end (dark bars). Some instructions are more useful for self-copying than others, so natural selection amplifies them disproportionately.

In a random soup, all 7 instructions appear in roughly equal amounts (~1/256 each, or about 0.39% of all bytes). After millions of interactions, the distribution shifts dramatically. Instructions involved in copying and pointer movement tend to increase, while others may stay flat or decrease.

What to look for: A massive increase in . (copy) and movement instructions (> <) is the signature of a replicator. Loop instructions ([ ]) are also critical — they provide the control flow needed to repeat copying operations.

06 — Phase Transition

The four signals of emergence

We track four metrics over time. When all three life criteria are met simultaneously — instruction density above 10%, replicator fraction above 5%, and compression ratio below 0.8 — we say "life has emerged." The dashed red lines show these thresholds.

Entropy measures randomness (high = noise, low = structure). Instruction density is the fraction of bytes that are valid BrainFuck. Compression ratio shows how repetitive/structured the programs are (lower = more structured). Replicator fraction is how many programs are exact copies of others.
Entropy
—
Shannon entropy of the byte distribution. Random bytes have entropy ~8.0 (maximum disorder). As replicators take over, entropy drops because fewer distinct byte values appear. A sharp drop often coincides with the phase transition.
Instruction Density
—
Fraction of all bytes in the soup that are valid BrainFuck instructions. Random noise gives ~2.7% (7 valid out of 256 possible values). The 10% threshold (red dashed line) is a strong signal that code is accumulating faster than chance.
Compression Ratio
—
The ratio of compressed size to original size (using zlib). Random data is incompressible (~1.0). Structured, repetitive programs compress well (<0.8). This metric captures whether programs have developed internal organization, not just valid instructions.
Replicator Fraction
—
The fraction of programs in the pool that are exact byte-for-byte copies of at least one other program. In a random pool this is effectively 0%. Above 5% (red dashed line), we know replication is happening systematically, not by coincidence.
07 — Life Criteria

Did life emerge?

Three conditions must be met simultaneously for us to declare that "life" has emerged in the soup. Each bar below shows the final value of a key metric against its threshold. Green means the criterion is met; grey means it is not.

What to look for: All three bars turning green. If even one criterion is not met, we can't say life emerged in a meaningful sense — there might be structure without replication, or copies without real code. All three together is the signal.
08 — Species

From diversity to dominance

At the start, every program is unique (the gold line is at the top). As replicators emerge, diversity collapses — a few "species" take over the entire population. The red dashed line shows how many programs are copies.

Think of it like an ecosystem. At first, every organism is a unique random creature — a million one-of-a-kind species. Then one species learns to reproduce. It spreads exponentially. Soon, what was once a diverse (but meaningless) wilderness becomes a monoculture of self-copiers. The collapse of the gold line is the extinction of randomness. The rise of the red line is the birth of life.

What to look for: A sharp "scissors" moment where the gold line (unique programs) drops while the red line (replicator copies) rises. This is the phase transition — the moment order conquers chaos. In many runs, this happens suddenly, not gradually.

09 — Before / After

Noise becomes code

Here's a direct comparison of a single program tape at the start (random initialization) versus the dominant replicator at the end. Each cell is one byte. Color tells you the instruction type.

A "healthy" replicator tape will be almost entirely colored — very few grey (noise) cells. It will typically show a repeating pattern: move the pointer, copy a byte, move again, copy again. The loop instructions (red) create the cycle that repeats this copying across the whole tape. If you see a tape that's mostly blue, green, and gold with red brackets, you're looking at a self-replicating program that assembled itself from nothing.

Color key: Blue = movement (> <), Green = arithmetic (+ -), Red = loops ([ ]), Gold = copy (.), Grey = noise (not a valid instruction). A healthy replicator will be mostly colored, not grey.
